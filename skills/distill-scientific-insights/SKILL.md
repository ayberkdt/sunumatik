# Distill Scientific Insights

Build an accurate importance map before shortening the material. Do not confuse what is easy to explain with what is scientifically central.

## Establish the audience decision

Determine what the audience should understand, believe, compare, remember, or decide after the presentation. Record prior knowledge, likely misconceptions, available time, and the cost of omitting context.

## Map the source

Separate research question, established context, method, evidence, result, interpretation, limitation, implication, and open question. Preserve observation versus inference and finding versus recommendation.

Read references/importance-model.md when the source is broad, technical, or internally repetitive.

## Rank the insights

Score candidate ideas by:

- necessity for the final conclusion;
- strength and uniqueness of supporting evidence;
- explanatory leverage over later ideas;
- relevance to the audience's decision;
- risk of misunderstanding if omitted;
- novelty relative to what the audience already knows.

Do not rank an idea highly only because it is surprising, visually dramatic, or easy to turn into a slogan.

## Build the hierarchy

Classify content as essential, supporting, appendix, or omit. Keep three to seven essential ideas for a normal talk. Identify prerequisite concepts and order them before dependent claims.

For every essential idea return a plain-language meaning, evidence link, epistemic status, audience relevance, misconception risk, and the minimum context required to understand it.

## Hand off

Return an insight brief containing the central question, one-sentence throughline, ranked insight map, dependency order, exclusions, appendix candidates, and unresolved ambiguities.

Pass the brief to structure-scientific-narrative for slide roles and to craft-scientific-storytelling for presentation rhythm. Do not write final slide copy in this skill.
